A homeowners policy does **not** cover flood. That surprise still catches Central Texas buyers and owners every year — especially after a creek comes out of its banks following a fast storm on already-saturated ground.

## Flood maps are a starting point, not the whole story

FEMA flood maps classify Special Flood Hazard Areas (typically A and V zones) where lenders require flood insurance for a mortgage. If your home is in a high-risk zone, flood coverage is not optional for the loan.

Plenty of damaging floods happen outside those lines. Maps lag new development, drainage changes, and the reality of Texas flash flooding. Homes in X zones (moderate or low risk) have no federal *mandate* — and still flood. Nationally, a large share of NFIP claims come from properties that were not in high-risk zones.

In Travis, Williamson, Hays, and surrounding counties, localized flooding along creeks, in low-lying streets, and near the Highland Lakes is well documented even when a house is not in a mapped floodplain.

## How flood insurance works

Most residential flood policies in the U.S. are written through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP), with some private flood options depending on the property. Coverage is typically split between:

- **Building coverage** — the structure and attached items
- **Contents coverage** — personal property, which you must often add separately

Waiting periods usually apply (commonly 30 days for NFIP unless the policy is tied to a loan closing), so flood insurance is not something you can bind the night before a forecasted storm.

## Who should strongly consider it

Talk with an advisor about flood coverage if any of these apply:

- The property is in a FEMA high-risk zone (often required by the lender)
- The house sits near a creek, lake, or known drainage path
- Neighbors have flooded, even if your map says “low risk”
- You are buying in a newly developed area where maps may not reflect current runoff
- You could not comfortably rebuild or replace belongings after water enters the home

The premium for a moderate-risk property is often far lower than people expect, and it is usually cheaper than recovering from a single uninsured water event.

## Pair it with your homeowners policy

Flood, sewer backup, and sudden plumbing leaks are different perils. A homeowners policy may cover certain water *inside* the home (a burst pipe) while excluding flood from surface water or overflow of a body of water. Backup of sewers and drains is often an endorsement, not a given.
